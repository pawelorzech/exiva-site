Privacy Policy

1. Summary

Exiva is an unofficial fan-made app for Tibia players. It is designed to work without collecting data about you.

  • If you use the free tier, we do not create an account for you and we hold no record about you in our server database. Tracked characters, notes and settings stay on your phone.
  • We never ask for your Tibia login credentials and we never will. All character information comes from publicly available sources.
  • We show no ads and we pass your data to nobody for marketing purposes.
  • We do not sell data. Ever.
  • We do not collect your phone number, location, contacts, photos, or device identifiers such as IMEI.
  • Signing in with Google is optional. You can buy and use the subscription without signing in.

6.1. Analytics requires your consent — why, and how we obtain it

Since 10 November 2024 Poland has applied the Electronic Communications Law of 12 July 2024 (Prawo komunikacji elektronicznej, Journal of Laws 2024 item 1221), which replaced Art. 173 of the old Telecommunications Law. The governing provision is Art. 399 PKE: storing information, or gaining access to information already stored, in a user's terminal equipment is permitted only if the user has been informed in advance about the purpose and about how to manage this in settings, and — after receiving that information — has given consent (Art. 399(1)(1)–(2)). Art. 399(3) exempts what is strictly necessary to transmit a communication or to deliver a service explicitly requested by the user.

We checked whether our analytics fits that exemption. It does not. Three things decided it:

  1. The rule is technology-neutral and covers apps, not just browsers. It speaks of "information" in "terminal equipment", not of cookies. The European Data Protection Board confirms this for mobile apps and SDKs: instructing the device to send stored information — including through an SDK — is "gaining access" within Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, version 2.0 adopted 7 October 2024, paras 34, 36 and 63).
  2. The Firebase app instance ID is written to the device and then sent off it. Had the app used it purely locally, it would fall outside the rule (EDPB Guidelines 2/2023, para 44). It goes to Google with every event, so it is inside.
  3. Analytics is not "necessary" to deliver the service you asked for. What you asked for is Tibia character tracking, not measurement of your behaviour. The Article 29 Working Party settled that first-party analytics is not exempt, and recommended a legislative exemption that was never enacted (Opinion 04/2012, WP194). Poland has adopted nothing equivalent to the French CNIL practice permitting consent-free audience measurement — and Firebase would not meet its criteria anyway.

Conclusion: yes, a consent screen is needed at first launch, before Firebase Analytics initialises at all. What is not needed is a heavyweight IAB-style consent platform — we have no advertising partners, so consent covers a single binary purpose.

How Exiva implements this:

  • Analytics and crash reporting are off by default — firebase_analytics_collection_enabled is set to false in the app manifest, so the SDK collects nothing before you decide.
  • At first launch we show one screen stating who (YesWas | Paweł Orzech), what is stored on the device (a pseudonymous app instance identifier), what for (usage statistics and crash diagnostics), who receives it (Google as processor), and that you can change it any time in Settings.
  • Two buttons of equal visual weight: "Agree" and "Decline". Nothing is pre-selected. Declining takes one tap and does not limit the app — making app use conditional on it would stop the consent being freely given.
  • Withdrawal is as easy as giving: Settings → Privacy, one toggle. Turning it off stops collection by the SDK itself (not merely our own events) and resets the app instance identifier.
  • We record the consent text, its version and the timestamp, so that consent can be evidenced.

Art. 399 PKE is supervised by the President of UKE, not the data protection authority — a separate track from the GDPR with its own liability (Art. 444 PKE).

Implementation note. Default-off collection and the consent screen are changes in the app's code, not merely in this document. Until they ship, the app should not go to the store — today it starts with analytics enabled and the settings toggle behaves as an opt-out, which is precisely what Art. 399(1)(2) forbids.

8. Data from public sources

Exiva retrieves information about characters, guilds, worlds, houses and highscores from publicly available sources — primarily the TibiaData API (api.tibiadata.com), which processes data published on the official tibia.com website. Encyclopaedic data comes from TibiaWiki and is made available under the CC BY-SA 3.0 licence. Full list of sources: ATTRIBUTIONS.md.

An important distinction:

We do not collect this data about you — we fetch it because you asked us to. When you enter a character name, the app queries a public source for it. We build no profile of any person from this, we do not link characters to player identities, and we share such combinations with nobody.

A character name is public information, but we treat it carefully. A character name is visible on tibia.com and is in itself a pseudonym. However, combined with your device or account it may indirectly indicate who you are in the game and which characters are yours. Therefore:

  • in our database character names appear primarily as irreversible hashes;
  • full names are stored only inside the encrypted copy of your data;
  • character names are never written to logs in plain text;
  • we do not and will not build a feature showing who tracks which characters.

We never ask for Tibia login credentials. Exiva has no game login screen, accepts no passwords, does not integrate with the game client and does not automate gameplay. If you ever see a screen claiming to be Exiva and asking for your Tibia password — that is not us. Report it to pawel@orzech.me.

14.1. The age threshold in Poland is 16 — checked, not assumed

Art. 8(1) GDPR sets 16 years as the age at which a child may consent to information society services, and lets Member States lower it to no less than 13.

On 2026-07-27 we checked the full text of the Polish Personal Data Protection Act of 10 May 2018 (Journal of Laws 2018 item 1000) in the Sejm's official repository (api.sejm.gov.pl/eli/acts/DU/2018/1000). The Act contains no provision lowering that threshold — its Art. 8 concerns appointing a data protection officer, not children. Poland therefore applies 16 years, straight from Art. 8(1) GDPR.

What the threshold governs, and what it does not. It governs only processing based on consent, for information society services offered directly to a child. It does not govern processing necessary to perform a contract (Art. 6(1)(b)) or based on legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f)). In Exiva it therefore bites on exactly one thing: consent to analytics and crash diagnostics (section 6.1) — which is the only thing we ask consent for.

A second, independent threshold: capacity to contract. A subscription is a paid contract. A person aged 13 to 18 has limited capacity under Art. 15 of the Polish Civil Code, and incurring an obligation requires the consent of their legal guardian (Art. 17). That is separate from the GDPR and does not go away at a lower data-protection age.

14.2. The Google Play declaration needs correcting

The planned "Target audience" declaration in Google Play Console is 13+. Against a 16-year threshold that is inconsistent with this document and with the consent design in section 6.1 — for a Polish user aged 13 to 15, consent to analytics would have to be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, and we have no mechanism to verify such consent and do not intend to build one.

The matter is more serious than it looks. Google Play's rules state that declaring an age band covering under-18s obliges you to consider whether they count as children locally, and apps targeting children must comply with the Google Play Families Policy. Google also states outright that misrepresenting the target audience may result in the app being removed or suspended (support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9285070, checked 2026-07-27). The "13–15" and "16–17" bands are themselves flagged as possibly including children in some locales.

On top of that sits a third, independent layer: capacity to contract. A subscription contract concluded by a person aged 13 to 17 without their legal guardian's consent is in suspended ineffectiveness (Art. 18 § 1 Civil Code) — and Art. 18 § 3 provides that the trader may not invoke the absence of that consent. The exception in Art. 20 (contracts commonly concluded in minor everyday matters) does not stretch to a renewing subscription.

Recommendation: declare a target audience of 18+ in Google Play and enable "Restrict Minor Access". It is the only configuration in which all three layers agree: no Families Policy, no Art. 8 GDPR, no Art. 17/18 Civil Code defect, and Google itself enforces the age floor at purchase and at subscription renewal.

The minimum the GDPR alone requires is 16+ — but 16+ solves neither the capacity-to-contract problem on a paid subscription nor the Families Policy exposure.

Until the publisher decides, this remains the only divergence between this document and the planned store declaration, and it must be resolved before publication. Section 14 above states a 16-year threshold and will need updating if the publisher chooses 18+.
